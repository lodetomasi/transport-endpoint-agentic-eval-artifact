\subsection{The grid}

Four models, each served by both Databricks and Amazon Bedrock under two transports: sixteen cells. The platforms are named rather than abstracted. The venue is single-blind, and the mechanisms of
\S\ref{sec:census} quote the endpoints verbatim, so a reader recognises the platform from the message
whatever we call it and can re-issue each probe against the platform that produced it. Every cell runs
the same 45 held-out binaries eight times, so the design plans 5{,}760 trials while the isolated-workspace
re-collection delivers 5{,}809 valid measurements and the original collection 5{,}805. The three numbers
differ for one reason: a run returning an infrastructure failure is logged as a record and \emph{re-run},
so a cell can end with more valid measurements than the 360 it planned. Every cell of both batches carries
all 45 binaries; the surplus --- 49 above plan in one batch and 45 in the other --- is the count of runs an
endpoint refused or dropped, which \S\ref{sec:census} treats as the subject rather than as attrition. One
binary of one cell ends at seven valid runs of eight rather than 45 binaries of 45, for the reason given in
\S\ref{sec:ottavo}. Turn budget, temperature and output cap are identical in every cell and recorded per
row rather than assumed. The re-collection cost \$139.40 across the sixteen cells, from \$1.22 to \$21.90
per cell, and every row carries its own cost so the price of any number in this paper is auditable beside
it.

\subsection{Why we say \emph{nominal} model}
\label{sec:nominal}

Throughout we write \emph{nominal model} rather than \emph{the same weights}. Two managed clouds
advertising the same open-weights model may serve different quantisations, different runtime versions, or
route to a different variant under load, and neither exposes enough to settle it --- a divergence already
measured directly, on 11 of 31 commercial endpoints for four Llama models~\citep{gao2025equality}. What we hold fixed is
therefore the model \emph{identity a user selects}, which is exactly what a published evaluation names in
its table --- and if two services selling one name behave differently, that difference belongs in the
method section of every paper that names the model and not the service, whatever its cause.

\subsection{Hypotheses, and why two of them are disjunctive}
\label{sec:ipotesi}

Both factors are manipulated \emph{as deployed}: a transport is a format together with the context volume
that format costs (\S\ref{sec:threats}), and an endpoint is a serving stack together with whatever weights
it actually loads (\S\ref{sec:nominal}). The hypotheses quantify over those compounds, not over idealised
single-variable versions no experiment on managed clouds can isolate. We ask whether the transport moves
the score at fixed infrastructure (H1), whether the infrastructure moves it at fixed transport (H2),
whether the transport's effect is constant across models (H3), and whether the two interact (H4). An
interval that excludes effects above some magnitude is not the same claim as no difference, and the second is never
written where the data support only the first.

\subsection{The test family, and why its size does not shrink}
\label{sec:family}

Ten tests were pre-registered as one family with Holm step-down, and the family size is fixed. It does not
shrink when a cell turns out to be inconclusive, uninteresting or unexecutable, because removing a member
after seeing the data lowers the thresholds of every survivor --- a correction growing more permissive
exactly as one learns which tests one would like to pass. The deliberate consequence is that contrasts
nobody disputes consume correction budget, which is the price of fixing $m$ before the data exist.

\subsection{Six defaults inherited from the companion study}

Six constraints are inherited from the companion study~\citep{companion2026baseline}, each traceable to
a defect that produced a clean and false number: unwrapped candidate source, functions in native order, symbol tables stripped,
a reasoning-only turn not recorded as a zero, a price guard that refuses \emph{before} the provider
call, and the token-limit parameter sent under the one name the endpoint accepts. None is a discovery
--- each is a default a competent implementer would set, which is what makes the list worth publishing
--- and the artifact states each with the defect it answers.
